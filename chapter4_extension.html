4.1 Hackathon Hustle

My first hackathon was the Tantra Hackathon at Savitribai Phule University, Pune. The rule was simple but tough: build a fast website without any AI tools or external assets — only from scratch.

Teamed up with my partner Satyam, we became a two-person coding machine. In just six intense hours, we built a medicine buyer website — pure, clean, and fully functional. No templates, no help. Just two screens, relentless focus, and seamless teamwork.

When the judges came to evaluate, they were visibly impressed. "You built this in six hours?" one asked, surprised. We won first prize.

4.1.1 The Notice Board Discovery

My hackathon life did not start with code; it started with a poster. Some senior had pinned a hand-made advertisement on the department notice board, between a workshop notice and a lost ID card appeal. "Build. Compete. Win." Three words, and the third one did all the work. I read that poster four times between two lectures, the way you re-read a message you cannot believe.

The details reached us properly through the class group, which is how all campus news actually travelled. Venue two hours away, registration free, food provided, teams of four. "Food provided" was the line that convinced my friends; free food is the most powerful marketing language in any college. We filled the form that same night, sitting in one room, typing each other's details because nobody trusted anybody else to spell their own name.

I did not know it then, but that grey notice board had quietly handed me a career. Everything that followed — the victories, the losses, the flights — began with one senior, one marker and three words. These days students scroll event platforms at midnight, and I feel fond of that old system. The algorithm shows you a hundred events. But one paper poster, read twice between lectures, still beats them all.

4.1.2 The Teammate Hunt

Finding a hackathon team is a small scandal every time. I had a registration form open and one box left to fill: team members. My batch of sixty suddenly produced sixty brilliant excuses. One had a submission due, one had a family function, one simply stopped replying, and one asked whether the hackathon could be shifted to a convenient date for him. I typed and deleted my message to the group four times.

Rescue arrived from an unexpected department. A boy from mechanical engineering, whom I knew only from nodding at the photocopy shop, said yes in five minutes and brought his roommate along. Neither of them wrote code that could frighten anyone, but both could stay awake for two days, argue politely and carry three laptops without complaint. There I learnt the first law of hackathon teams: on the first day, commitment matters more than skill.

Over the years I have formed many hackathon teams, and I still evaluate people the same way. Skills can be built in one night before an event; hunger cannot be downloaded at all. The best teammate I ever found was not the smartest coder in the batch. He was simply the one who never once asked, "yaar, why are we doing this?"

4.1.3 The Three-Day Syllabus

With the team confirmed, a new terror appeared: what exactly would we build there? Seniors gave a terrifying answer — you cannot learn everything, learn one thing properly. So we held an emergency meeting and wrote a syllabus of our own: one framework, one database, one design tool. Three days. Nobody assigned it; nobody would test it. It was the first syllabus I ever studied with full hunger.

Our preparation had its own comedy. We downloaded tutorial videos like soldiers stocking rations, printed shortcut keys for the wall, and gave each member one weapon: I took the frontend, mechanical boy took the database, his roommate took the presentation, and the fourth member appointed himself head of snacks. Division of labour, we called it proudly. The presentation boy then asked the deepest question of the week: what if judges ask something outside the syllabus? We sent him back to finish the syllabus first.

That self-made syllabus taught me a method I still use before anything new: shrink the ocean to a glass, then drink it fully. Courses and degrees spread knowledge thin across three hundred pages. A deadline of three days concentrates it. When I later trained juniors for their first hackathon, I gave them the same gift — a small list, a short deadline, and full permission to ignore the rest.

4.1.4 The Long Bus Ride

The hackathon was two hours away, in a college whose name we could not pronounce correctly on the first attempt. We boarded the early morning bus with backpacks, laptops and one shared power bank, looking like a very small startup going on its first offsite. Somewhere near the third town, we realised none of us had eaten breakfast, which is how most engineering adventures officially begin.

The journey turned into an unplanned team meeting. We revised our plan on the back of the registration receipt, argued about the project name for forty minutes, and practised introducing ourselves like professionals. One teammate slept against the window the entire way and woke up only when the conductor tapped his shoulder for the ticket — a boy who had planned a database for three days but had forgotten to plan for a ticket.

I have travelled to many hackathons since — some by train, some by late-night buses, once by three different vehicles in a single day. The destination always justified the journey, but the journey itself became the part I remember best. There is something about moving towards a challenge with your team that loosens every tongue. By the time the college gate arrived, four strangers had become one team, and the event had not even started.

4.1.5 The Registration Desk

College gates at hackathon mornings have a festival feel that no regular lecture day ever achieved. Banners, confused volunteers, seniors directing traffic with hand gestures, and a queue of students from different cities comparing laptops like jeeps before a safari. We joined the line at the registration desk, where a volunteer crossed our names off a list with the seriousness of airport security.

Then came my favourite part of every hackathon: the welcome kit. An ID card with a lanyard, a t-shirt that never fit anyone perfectly, a pen, a notepad and stickers. I wore that ID card for the rest of the day even when it served no purpose, because it made me feel official. In the photographs from that morning, all four of us are wearing our lanyards like Olympic medals.

Inside the kit there was also a schedule sheet, and one line on it changed how I see events: stay hydrated and take breaks. I had never seen a college event care about its participants before, and I still remember which event printed that line. When I later helped organise hackathons of our own, we printed the same line on our sheets. People forget the prize list within a month. They remember for years how an event treated them at the door.

4.1.6 The Rookies in the Room

The hall sorted itself into two species within the first hour: the veterans and us. Veterans arrived with team hoodies, laptops covered in conference stickers, and a frightening calm. They greeted the organisers by first name and discussed past finals like old soldiers discussing old campaigns. We had fresh t-shirts, unmatched enthusiasm and a plan that fit on half a page. Nobody warned us that we were outnumbered by experience.

Being rookies had its own advantages, which I discovered slowly. We asked questions that experienced teams had grown too proud to ask. We followed the schedule honestly, while the veterans skipped meals like it proved their dedication. When a mentor visited our table, he spent forty minutes with us — partly because we were weak, partly because we were the only team taking notes while he spoke.

That day ended without a prize, and it remains one of my favourite hackathons. The veterans taught me more by example than any workshop could: how they divided work, how they tested early, how they spoke to judges. Rookies are not behind; they are simply at the beginning of a longer race. Every veteran in that hall had once sat at a table with half a plan, and every future victory of mine was quietly seeded in that first, clumsy, completely hopeful morning.

4.2.1 The Rules That Banned Shortcuts

The Tantra Hackathon at Savitribai Phule Pune University arrived with a rule sheet that read like a strict teacher's wish list: build fast, build a working website, and build it without AI tools or borrowed assets — nothing from outside, everything from scratch. My first reaction was fear. My second was a laugh. My third, somewhere between the two, was respect; somebody had designed a format where only honest skill could speak.

That rule sheet quietly rewrote our preparation. For the first time, copy-paste — the unofficial fifth subject of every engineering degree — was banned, and we had to check what remained in our hands. Satyam and I spent an evening writing syntax by hand on blank pages, no editor to correct us, like cricketers practising without pads. Whatever survived that evening was ours. Whatever did not, we studied again.

I understand the organisers better now. AI tools are excellent servants, but on that day they would have been fake muscles — the event wanted to know what we could lift alone. Rules that remove shortcuts feel cruel in the beginning and generous by the end, because they hand you back a rare thing: proof. Six hours later we had built something no search engine could claim credit for.

4.2.2 The Partner and the Plan

Some hackathons need five members; Tantra rewarded pairs, and Satyam was the easiest yes of my student life. We had studied in the same class long enough to finish each other's sentences and each other's bugs. A two-person team is a marriage of the technical kind: no middlemen, no committees, no waiting for votes. One nod, and the work simply moved from his screen to mine.

We divided the six hours like a marriage divides a kitchen: quietly, and with full trust. Satyam took the backend logic — accounts, orders, the database spine — and I took the frontend, the face the judges would meet. We chose a medicine buyer website: simple to explain, honest to build, and useful in a way our families would understand immediately. No grand ideas. Just one clean problem, solved properly.

That partnership taught me what people later call collaboration, a word I distrust until I see it working. Working with Satyam was not two people typing side by side; it was one brain wearing two keyboards. He wrote a function, I already knew its shape. I broke something, he felt it before I confessed. Since then I judge every team by one silent test: how little does the team need to explain itself?

4.2.3 The Clock on the Wall

Six hours sounds generous until you watch it move. We spent the first twenty minutes only planning — a crime by sprint standards, and the best investment of the day. One page, three columns: must build, should build, only if time smiles. The hall around us was already typing; we were still writing. It felt like being left behind on purpose, and every sprint since has rewarded that patience.

We agreed on one strange rule: no talking while the code was flowing. Questions went into a shared notepad; answers came at the next checkpoint, every ninety minutes. To an outsider we looked antisocial, two students glued to separate screens. Inside the silence there was a rhythm — write, test, sync, breathe — and the project grew like dough left alone to rise.

The final hour had a law of its own: nothing new enters the build. No feature however tempting, no redesign however beautiful. We spent it testing what existed, polishing labels and button positions, and rehearsing the demo twice. Beginners chase ambition; veterans chase completeness. That frozen last hour is the reason our demo never once froze. I still follow it in every deadline of my life.

4.2.4 Hour Four, One Letter

Around hour four, the disaster arrived wearing a very small dress: one bug. A customer could fill the whole medicine cart, but the place-order button simply refused to move. We clicked it ten times, the way people press a lift button harder as if that makes the lift arrive faster. The no-talking rule collapsed instantly, and our shared notepad filled with sentences that were no longer professional. My hands had gone cold although the hall was warm.

We returned to an ancient method: we stopped typing and started reading. Satyam read his backend aloud, slowly, like reciting a prayer, and I walked the same path through my frontend with a finger on the screen. Somewhere in the middle the two paths disagreed. His code called it order; mine called it orders. One plural letter was sitting quietly between us, poisoning the entire pipeline. The fix took eight seconds. The finding took forty minutes.

That hour taught me something no classroom had managed: pressure does not create bugs, it only finds the ones already sleeping in your code. After the fix we did not celebrate or high-five; we simply resumed typing, strangely calmer than before. Even today, whenever a project breaks close to a deadline, I hear hour four in my head. Stay quiet, follow the flow, and remember the villain is usually one careless word.

4.2.5 The Judge and the Mouse

By hour five the organisers announced judging, and the hall changed its weather. Judges moved from table to table with scorecards, and every team suddenly became an actor caught without rehearsal. We watched three demos before ours: one team presenting confident slides about a project they had not finished, another where the app crashed live on stage. Satyam whispered that at least our product was alive. That is the strange comfort of honest building — you fear less, because you have less to hide.

When our turn came, we followed a rule we had set while rehearsing: hand the judge the mouse and step back. He registered himself as a user, searched a medicine, added it to the cart and placed an order without our help — the whole journey in under two minutes. He asked how we had divided the work, and then, scrolling back to the first screen, asked the question I still remember word for word: you built this in six hours?

Satyam and I exchanged the kind of look that needs no translation, and answered, almost together, yes, sir. The judge wrote something on his sheet without changing his face, thanked us and moved on. We had prepared a whole speech about our vision, and in the end the product had spoken instead. Since that day my demo style has stayed the same: keep the speech short, hand over the controls, and trust what we built.

4.2.6 First Prize, First Spark

The results came at the end, as results always do, when everybody is too tired to feel anything. Then they read our names for first prize, and tiredness left the building. Satyam grabbed my shoulder; I forgot how legs work for a second. We walked up, received a certificate and a small trophy, and posed for a photograph with smiles too wide for two exhausted faces.

People assume the prize was the best part. Honestly, the trophy gathered dust on a shelf within months. The real prize was quieter: the proof that two students with no template, no AI and no senior to call could build something judges ranked first. That evening I updated nothing anywhere online, but I updated something far more important — my own belief about what pressure can produce.

Most of all, that win hooked me — not on trophies, but on the specific taste of turning pressure into possibility. Satyam and I did not become a permanent team; life eventually moved us to different cities, as life does. But the Tantra sprint remains our private monument. Six hours, two screens, one website, zero shortcuts. Whenever a new challenge looks impossible, I return to that evening and remember: we were ordinary students, and it worked.

Our first 24-hour national hackathon changed everything. We built a project in the healthcare sector — solving, coding, surviving on coffee and chaos. When the results were announced, they called runner-ups first, then the second winner. My heart was pounding. Then they announced the first prize winners: Team Tech Tribe.

For a moment, I just sat there — shocked, frozen. My teammates erupted: screaming, jumping, hugging. I was still in my chair, trying to process it. We had just won ₹ 1,00,000. "Lakhpati ban gaye, guru!"

That victory didn't just fill our pockets — it filled us with the belief that we could build, compete, and win on a national stage.

4.3.1 The National Leap

Tantra had made us confident, so we did what confident people do: we registered for a twenty-four-hour national hackathon without reading the fine print. The participant list arrived by email and humbled us immediately. Teams from Kerala, Delhi, Gujarat, Hyderabad — some travelling with faculty coordinators, some with sponsor logos on their t-shirts. We had a Google Form confirmation and one shared bag of expectations. The national tag was not decoration; the room actually felt like India.

Our first calculation was innocent: six hours had made us winners, so twenty-four would make us super-winners. Wrong, the briefing explained gently. A full-day event is not four sprints stacked together; it is a marathon that tests sleep, food and temperament along with code. Prototypes by dawn, pitches by noon, no leaving the venue. Our team, Tech Tribe, had been named in the final minute of registration because every decent name was already taken. Now that joke of a name had to survive a national stage.

I have noticed a pattern since: every jump in scale rewrites the map. What felt mastered at one level becomes a beginner's shuffle at the next, and that is not an insult, it is a syllabus. That morning I was simultaneously proud of my first prize and quietly terrified of the hall around me. Both feelings were correct. Confidence opens doors, but a little fear keeps your eyes open while you walk through them.

4.3.2 A Problem From Home

When the problem statements opened, we scanned the list the way hungry people scan a menu. Smart cities, agriculture, education — and one quiet line about healthcare. That line pulled me somewhere personal. Months earlier, a relative of ours had fallen seriously ill at night, and I had watched the family telephone five hospitals one after another, hunting for a bed, taking addresses, hanging up, dialling again. Nobody had told us technology could remove exactly this kind of night.

So our project chose itself: one screen that shows which nearby hospitals have beds free right now, updated by the hospitals themselves, searchable by a panicked relative at two in the morning. We argued about features, then cut most of them; in twenty-four hours, a small working truth beats a large imaginary one. The design carried a single rule: a frightened user should never have to think.

That day taught me where good problems actually live: not in lists, but in houses. Any team could have picked the healthcare line; only our table carried that particular night inside it. When you build for a person you actually know, every button has a face, and it shows in the final product. Judges, I later learnt, can smell the difference between a borrowed idea and an owned one from across the hall.

4.3.3 Three in the Morning

No briefing truly prepares you for a hackathon hall at three in the morning. The music stopped, the announcements stopped, even the coffee machine queue shrank to the truly desperate. Half the hall had surrendered, heads resting on backpacks, and the other half was typing with the silent, red-eyed loyalty of night-shift workers. A volunteer moved between tables like a nurse on rounds, refilling water bottles and whispering that breakfast would appear at five.

We had planned for this night with a shift system: two of us slept for three hours while two built, then we rotated. The sleeping pair lay right under the table on the spare banners organisers had left on the floor. The joke of those hours is that they were our most productive — no announcements, no wanderers, no self-inflicted debates. Some of our best decisions, including the colour of the entire interface, were made in whispers at three in the morning.

That night corrected a belief I had carried from the movies — that great work belongs to people who never sleep. In a twenty-four-hour event, the team that manages rest builds better at hour twenty than the team that glorifies exhaustion. Stamina, I realised, is not a personality trait; it is a design decision. We were not the loudest team in that hall, but when the sun came up, we were among the steadiest.

4.3.4 Five Minutes on Stage

By noon the hall had transformed into an auditorium, and one by one teams climbed the stage to pitch before a judges' panel that actually looked national — industry badges, serious notebooks, water bottles arranged symmetrically. Five minutes per team, a buzzer at four. We had rehearsed our pitch eleven times in a corridor, and I still felt my knees negotiating with the stairs as we walked up under the lights.

Our speaker opened beautifully, reached the second memorised sentence, and stopped. Three full seconds of silence on a stage feels like three semesters. Then our quietest teammate — the one who had built the database and never once volunteered for talking — took one step forward and simply explained the product the way he would explain it to a friend. No polish, no drama, just truth with a demo behind it. The audience relaxed; honesty, it turns out, is an excellent orator.

Since that day I have stopped worshipping rehearsed lines. A memorised pitch is like glass — impressive until something touches it — while understanding bends and survives. The judges' questions afterwards were answered by three different voices, because all three of us actually knew what we had built. That, I decided, would be my forever rule for presentations: rehearse the flow, never the sentences, and make sure every hand on the laptop can hold the microphone.

4.3.5 Team Tech Tribe

The closing ceremony began with the cruelty all result ceremonies share: they call the smaller prizes first. Runner-ups were announced — not us. Second winner — not us. Around me, teams clapped politely while quietly crossing their fingers, and I did that strange mathematics of hope: if our name had not appeared in the small prizes, only two possibilities remained, and one of them was too large to say aloud. My heart was pounding like it wanted to leave.

Then the anchor said the words: the first prize goes to Team Tech Tribe. My teammates erupted instantly — screaming, jumping, one of them hugging a complete stranger from the next row. I stayed exactly where I was, sitting, blinking, trying to process the arithmetic: one, followed by five zeroes. One lakh rupees. I walked to the stage on legs I had borrowed from somewhere, and held the giant cheque every photograph demands.

People sometimes ask why I did not jump like the others. Honestly, the news took the scenic route to my brain. Wanting to win is a fire you learn to carry quietly, but hearing that you won is a sound your body does not have a file for. Even today that announcement plays back in slow motion — the anchor's pause, our team name, the zeroes arranging themselves in the air. Some moments refuse to become memories; they stay present tense.

4.3.6 The Days After

The next few days felt like a dream someone else was directing. Our photo appeared in a newspaper — slightly blurred, all of us mid-laugh — and the clipping travelled through family WhatsApp groups faster than any project we had ever built. Relatives who had never once asked what I study suddenly called to ask about this hackathon thing. My mother answered the same call five times and gave the same tour of the newspaper each time. I let her. Some audiences you never interrupt.

The prize money itself behaved like an adult for the first week: we split it equally, formally, like founders. But the amount was never the real story. What filled us was rarer — the proof that an idea born on a hostel floor could stand on a national stage and be counted first. Validation is a strange fuel. It does not make you richer, it makes you louder in your own head, and that voice kept saying: we can do this again.

Looking back, I understand that day differently now. Lakhpati ban gaye was the joke; the truth underneath was quieter — the day we received permission. Permission to believe that five zeroes could follow a one, that our small city and our small team belonged in national conversations. Many more hackathons followed that one, with far harder problems and far taller teams. But whenever a new attempt feels too big, I remember the cheque, the blurred photograph, and the arithmetic that refused to make sense until it finally did.

4.4.1 The Registration Spree

The lakh changed how the world said our name. Invitations that once needed hunting began arriving on their own — college fests, inter-university meets, a corporate challenge in the city with actual badges. Seniors who had once ignored our messages forwarded our photos with the caption, these kids are serious. Momentum, I discovered that month, is not a feeling. It is a tailwind that pushes every new registration form onto your desk half-filled.

So we did what victorious teams do: we registered for everything. Three events in one month, each form filled from the same saved document of team details. Our faculty coordinator, a patient man, signed no-objection letters so often that he kept one pen aside purely for us. Attendance stretched thinner than the hostel's tomato chutney, and the college allowed it, because the college had decided we were worth the risk.

Looking back, the spree was half strategy and half celebration. Momentum is real fuel, but it steers badly; it will carry you through every open door whether the room inside deserves you or not. Still, I would not change those crowded weeks. They taught us formats, judges and time limits in bulk, and they quietly collected the lesson I was about to need most — the one that only a loss can teach.

4.4.2 The Day Our Name Was Not Called

The spree's first stop was a big-city hackathon with corporate sponsors and a stage taller than any we had seen. We walked in wearing our national-winner confidence like an extra lanyard. For their theme of warehouse logistics, we confidently re-entered our hospital bed-finder — polished, battle-tested, beloved. The demo ran without one stutter. Somewhere on that highway, one of us finally said the sentence out loud: we were good, just not for this question. That loss taught me what all our wins had not — a hackathon is not an exam where knowledge alone passes; it is a question, and the first skill of the arena is answering the one that was actually asked. Since that day I read problem statements the way lawyers read contracts: twice, with a pencil.

4.4.3 The Chart Paper Constitution

The loss stung enough to become useful. That night in the hostel we held a post-mortem where nobody was allowed to defend themselves, and by midnight a chart paper hung on our wall like a constitution: pick a problem with a face, build a demo that runs in ninety seconds, rehearse the pitch three times, ship small and finish, and test the product on one stranger before any judge sees it.

The constitution worked embarrassingly well. The next three events were smaller — a college fest, a civic problem meet, a city-level competition — and we answered each brief exactly as it was asked. A library-issue tracker. A bus timetable project. An attendance system. Three events, three first prizes. The trophies were modest enough to stack vertically, but the streak was real, and more real than that was the habit behind it.

Later we added one more column to the chart paper: a ledger of losses, with dates and honest reasons. Professionals, I understood eventually, are not people who never lose; they are people who convert losing into documents. Even today, every project I begin starts with a checklist that is, if you trace its bloodline carefully, that chart paper wearing a tie and sitting in a meeting.

4.4.4 The Poster With a Passport

Somewhere in the middle of the streak, an announcement rearranged the whole semester. A national innovation challenge, open to engineering teams from every state, with one line at the bottom of its poster that made the entire lab go quiet: Grand Finale — Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia. One of us read it aloud twice, the way you read news you do not believe, and then the lab stayed quiet for another full minute.

They were probably right, and their advice would have weighed more if our wall did not carry a rule the seniors could not see: we no longer entered events to be realistic. This poster asked one question — can you build something worth flying for? — and every one of us wanted to answer it.

Audacity, I have learnt, is cheap at the registration stage and expensive at the finale; that is the honest design of every large opportunity. We paid the small fee that evening — a form, our details, our best project — and walked back to the lab as if nothing had happened, though everything had. The chart paper on the wall had never mentioned passports. It did not need to; it had already trained us for the question.

4.4.5 The Gate After Every Gate

The challenge turned out to be a pyramid wearing a competition's clothes. First gate: an online round of videos and write-ups. Second: a zonal round in another city, forty teams in a hall, five minutes each before industry judges. Third: a prototype screening where the judges actually touched the product with their hands. Each gate removed half the crowd, and each gate kept its own particular flavour of fear.

Our wall constitution earned its keep at every level. The ninety-second demo slid neatly into the five-minute slots. We answered the zonal theme exactly as asked, no flourishes. At the prototype screening, a judge did something I still admire — he switched off the internet on purpose, then watched our bed-finder keep working through its offline SMS mode, the feature we had built for rural areas that nobody had requested. 4.6 Important Lessons of Life

Hackathons aren't just about winning. They're about growing, learning, failing, rising, and discovering stronger versions of ourselves. With every project, I earn something new. 4.6.1 The Degree the Arena Gave

People ask what hackathons actually teach, since no marksheet records them. My first answer is always deadline engineering. Every event hands you an impossible clock, and each event trains the same reflex: shrink the ocean to a glass, sort the work into must build, should build and only if time smiles, then drink the glass completely. A university spreads its syllabus across years. The arena concentrates a whole education into thirty-six hours.

The second skill has no name in any prospectus: finishing. Most students complete assignments; very few complete things. A hackathon ends with a demonstration in front of strangers whether you are ready or not, and that forced ending, repeated across events, builds a muscle no classroom can order online. In later years I watched brilliant people fail at good ideas for one reason only — they never learnt to stop polishing and start shipping.

My degree certificate carries a university's name and I am proud of it. But when the hard days of my working life arrived, the skills that actually reported for duty were the arena's: scoping, sequencing, testing early, freezing the last hour. If the arena ever issued degrees, I suspect the convocation would be held at three in the morning, with a one-line degree citation: this one finishes things.

4.6.2 The Teammate Algorithm

The arena's second syllabus was people. On the first day of any team, commitment outranks skill — the mechanical boy taught me that before he could write a database. Skill can be built overnight before an event; hunger cannot be downloaded at all. Having judged many events since, I can now diagnose a doomed team from the door by one symptom: somewhere in it, someone keeps asking yaar, why are we doing this?

The arena also taught me team size honestly. Tantra proved that two people with full trust can outrun five with committees. The national finale proved that three voices who each know the build will always out-pitch one memorised speech. My test for every team since has stayed the same: how little does this team need to explain itself? The best ones communicate in nods, shared notepads and single-word warnings about plural letters.

Everything I later understood about hiring, founding and collaborating was seeded in those halls. A team is a small machine for converting trust into work, and hackathons assemble that machine under a timer, which is the only honest test. The boy who carried three laptops without complaint, the partner who felt the bug before I confessed it, the self-appointed head of snacks — they all taught me one final rule: choose people by how they behave at hour twenty, not hour one.

4.6.3 The Syllabus of Losing

The arena's hardest subject was losing, and nobody could get an exemption. We lost to a theme we had ignored, to a demo that crashed live on a stage while ours sat finished in our bag, to teams with better slides and thinner products. Every loss carried its own tuition fee, and I kept the receipts — that chart paper ledger on the hostel wall, which later became a notebook, which later still became a habit that outlived the notebook.

Losing well, I learnt, is itself a discipline with steps. Feel it fully for one evening; pretending the prize did not matter poisons the lesson. Then the post-mortem, in writing, while the sting is still honest. Then one new rule onto the wall, so the same failure cannot invoice you twice. What quietly ruins teams is not losing — it is losing without paperwork and entering the next hall as the exact same team.

Years later that ledger became my best teaching material. Every junior I trained received the same sentence: the arena charges in losses, and it does not print receipts unless you keep them. My worst days now compound like savings. Some difficult project goes smoothly, some deadline dissolves instead of exploding, and I know precisely which lost evening funded that calm. Failure, documented, is the only investment that never asks for more capital.

If you asked me to compress this whole chapter into one line, it would be this: a poster became a passport.
