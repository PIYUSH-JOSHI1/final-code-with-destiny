2.1 All-Nighters & Submissions

The real chaos begins the night before a 12 p.m. deadline. Phones buzz nonstop, everyone's scrambling, asking, "Has anyone finished TAE-3?" Panic unites us all.

Texts fly: "Anyone done with TAE-3?" "I'm still on page one!" "Did you even start?" TAE 1, 2, or 3, they all forced us into this same last-minute rush. In those late-night scrambles, we weren't competing. We were just trying to make it, together.

Then, like a hero in pajamas, that one friend who finished early emerges. For a few hours, they're our king and savior. We huddle around their laptop, copying formulas, begging for explanations. They share not with pride, but with quiet grace, knowing next time roles may reverse. Years later, we won't recall the grades. We'll remember the shared panic, the relieved sigh at 11:57 a.m., and the friend who saved us all.

2.1.1 The Seven-Day Plan

Every submission began with a plan, and every plan began with the same sentence: this time I will start early. I wrote the schedule in my diary with ruler-drawn boxes — research on Monday, writing on Tuesday, printing on Wednesday, buffer days after that. The plan looked so responsible that I felt proud of it for six days. Then I counted, and the deadline was no longer a date on the calendar. It was a countdown.

The last night arrived with full drama. I spread my notes on the desk like a general preparing for war, made one strong chai for courage, and opened the laptop at nine. Ten pages were due by morning. The diary, with its beautiful boxes and honest intentions, had produced exactly one page and a good title. The title was excellent, actually. Even today I defend it in front of people who wrote all ten pages.

Years later I still smile at the seven-day plan, but I no longer call it failure. That plan taught me how my mind negotiates with time: it always promises future-me more discipline than present-me actually owns. So I changed the trick. I began setting myself fake deadlines five days early and hiding the real date like an exam answer. The habit still runs every project I take. The plan was never the problem. Trusting the plan was.

2.1.2 The Group Chat Storm

TAE-1, TAE-2, TAE-3 — the names still carry a small thunder. On submission nights our class group turned into a live news channel. Anyone done with TAE-3? I am still on page one. Bhai, did you even start? Three questions, sent by six people at once, and the panic multiplied with every buzz of the phone. We were not competing with each other. We were drowning together, loudly, in plain text.

The best part was the instant parliament that formed at midnight. Somebody shared a format. Somebody shared an old sample. Somebody who actually understood the topic recorded long voice notes that nobody played at full volume, in case the warden's footsteps came closer. Screenshots travelled like emergency rations. By two in the morning that group had produced more cooperation than one full semester of classroom team projects had managed.

Those chats are my favourite archaeology now. Scroll up in any old group and you find the night we all nearly failed together — and did not. Panic has a strange chemistry: it dissolves ego faster than friendship ever could. I asked doubts I would have been too proud to ask in daylight, and somebody answered at 2 a.m. without a single joke. Engineering taught me many subjects. The group chat taught me the most useful one: how to need people without shame.

2.1.3 The Hero in Pajamas

Every submission night produced exactly one hero, and he was always in pajamas. The friend who, for reasons still unknown to science, had finished early. He posted one calm line in the group — mine is done, call if you need — and for a few hours he became the most important person in the whole batch. Kings have ruled with less grace and more paperwork.

We huddled around his laptop copying formulas, and the surprising part was his patience. He explained every borrowed line without a trace of pride, as if he already knew that next month the roles would reverse and he would be the one begging. That is the unwritten contract of engineering submissions: today's hero is tomorrow's beggar. Everybody signs it on the first day, and nobody ever reads the fine print.

In second year the crown finally reached me. I finished early once, posted my one calm line, and spent the whole night explaining the same concept eleven times to eleven desperate friends. I was exhausted and strangely happy. That night I understood what our heroes had been quietly practising all along: teaching under pressure, at midnight, to people too tired to pretend they understood. No viva ever tested me as hard. No viva ever taught me as much.

2.1.4 The Printer Wars

The hostel had one working printer, and on submission mornings it became the most contested border in the country. By six a.m. a queue stood outside its owner's room, reports in hand, each of us holding exactly one chance of getting printed before the nine o'clock deadline. The machine jammed with the confidence of a government office. We waited anyway, because hope is free and A4 sheets are not.

The queue had its own constitution. Whoever was printing the last page of a fifty-page report earned respect. Whoever was printing the first page of a fifty-page report earned sympathy and a faster turn. Paper was borrowed and repaid in biscuits. Once, mid-jam, four engineers performed emergency surgery on that poor machine with a hairpin, and when it finally coughed out the page, we clapped like it had just landed a plane.

I own better printers today and trust none of them the way I trusted that hostel monster. It taught me the hidden syllabus of submission week: finishing the report is only half the job. The other half is the queue, the paper, the jam and the walk to class. Deadlines are never defeated by good content alone. They are defeated by logistics, biscuits and one well-timed clap.

2.1.5 The 3 A.M. Wall

Every all-nighter has a wall, and the wall always arrived at three in the morning. By then the chai had stopped working, the jokes had turned stupid, and one friend was laughing at a sentence that was not funny. We called it the three a.m. brain. It still types, but it no longer thinks. Any code written after that hour was basically fan fiction.

We learnt to respect the wall the hard way, and then we outsmarted it with shameless simplicity: naps in shifts. Twenty minutes each, one person guarding the alarm like a security guard at a museum. The one who slept woke up fresh and typed like a hero. The one who guarded produced the night's stupidest paragraph and took his turn next. We wrote better work with less pride and more sleep.

That wall taught me the most expensive lesson of my four years: endurance is not about staying awake. It is about managing energy. In every deadline since, when the work turns heroic, I remember the shifts. Sleep is not the enemy of the deadline; stupid decisions are. The engineers who survive all-nighters are not the ones who refuse to rest. They are the ones who rest in shifts and return with a working brain.

2.1.6 The Green Tick

The submission portal had one religion, and the god was a green tick. Uploaded successfully — two words that could heal an entire night. At 11:40 the batch refreshed the page together, forty students, one server, zero faith. The server always chose those exact minutes to become slow, as if it too had spent its semester submitting late and needed the drama.

I still remember my first green tick. My hands were shaking, and the file had been renamed four times in pure panic — final, final2, final_FINAL, final_final_true — before the upload bar began crawling like it was walking to college on foot. Then the tick appeared, and we screamed in the corridor at midnight, quietly. Results took weeks to arrive. The tick arrived instantly, and it forgave everything.

People assume engineers fear deadlines. Not true. We fear the forty minutes before them, and we live for the tick after them. That small green mark is the only honest medal of student life: it does not ask for your marks, your attendance or your confidence. It asks one question — did you make it or not? Years from now I may forget every grade I earned. I will not forget the tick.

2.2 Coding Everywhere

Real engineering life wasn't lived in classrooms — it was coded in claimed corners of the campus. From cafeteria tables and quiet staircases to shady spots under old trees, these unofficial labs turned boring benches into launchpads and forgotten library nooks into secret think tanks. This is where collaboration sparked without a schedule, and procrastination often ignited our most creative breakthroughs.

Whether we were building a silly robot at 2 a.m. in a cluttered hostel room, or huddled together on the open campus lawn under a blanket of stars, our best creations were born from beautiful chaos.

In those messy, laughter-filled moments, we didn't just code — we wrote memories, forged friendships, and sketched the story of who we were becoming.

2.2.1 The Canteen Classroom

The lab had timings; the canteen had none. Our table near the window slowly became an unofficial campus of its own — one laptop, three plates of samosas, and a compiler running between the ketchup and the notes. People imagine coding needs silence, a desk and perfect lighting. My best half-hour of coding ever happened next to a frying pan, with oil smoke drifting across the screen. Comfort is not a place; it is a mood. When the team laughs easily and the food is warm, the code behaves too. Every engineer should find a canteen table of their own and defend it gently.

2.2.2 The Staircase Cabin

Between the third floor and the fourth, our college kept its most productive office: a staircase landing with one window, one breeze and a permanent do-not-disturb sign written in nothing at all. Just six concrete steps, a wall to lean the laptop against, and a corner quiet enough for logic to speak. We called it the cabin, half as a joke and half with respect.

Questions asked on stairs get honest answers, because gravity does something to ego: everyone is literally at a different level, and nobody minds.

I still take calls on staircases when offices get too loud. Some part of that landing is installed in me permanently: work does not need a room, it needs a corner you trust. Titles, cabins and conference rooms came later in life; the staircase taught me first that a good workspace is any place where your mind stops performing and starts working. The rent was zero, and the view of the corridor beat any glass office I have sat in since.

2.2.3 The Train Build

Every long vacation train ride carried a passenger with a laptop and misplaced priorities: me. Somewhere between two stations, with my bag as a table and a stranger uncle judging my screen, I wrote the first version of a project that later won a college prize. 2.2.4 The Phone-Only Week

In second year, my laptop died a dramatic death — one spark, one smell, one funeral — and repairs took a week. Exams were close, and the practical file needed typing. So I did what desperate engineers do: I coded on my phone, thumbs racing on a keyboard meant for forwards and good morning messages, not for loops and brackets. Something unexpected happened in that week. With a tiny screen, there is no space for clever code, so I wrote the smallest version of everything. Short functions. Clear names. One idea per line. When the laptop returned and I copied the phone code onto the big screen, the teacher's comment was that my style had improved. The smallest screen taught the cleanest habit.

I have respected constraints ever since. Big screens hide sloppy habits; small screens expose them like a mirror. Now, when a design feels bloated, I imagine reading it on that phone keyboard, thumb by painful thumb. If it survives that test, it is simple enough to ship. My dead laptop cost a week. The week paid me back with a style.

2.2.5 The Silent Sprint

The library had one sacred rule — silence — and one group that refused to abandon it: us. We had a group submission due and a library slot booked, so we invented the silent sprint: six people, one table, zero spoken words. Everything travelled by notebook paper, passed under the table like secret school notes. A frown meant error. An arrow meant explain again.

Halfway through, the comedy peaked: our most talkative teammate, banned from speech for two hours, discovered handwriting. His notes got longer and politer than his voice ever was. By the end, the paper trail on our table looked like a small postal service, and we finished the submission with better focus than any loud session had ever produced. Silence, it turns out, is a team member too.

The silent sprint returns in my life every busy season. When talk becomes noise, I switch to writing — short messages, clear bullets, no meeting. Words spent talking are loud; words spent writing stay and work. Six students breaking a rule politely taught me a management system that no company has improved upon yet: write more, say less, and pass the paper.

2.2.6 The Portable Office

By final year, my laptop bag had become part of my body. The class demo was ready. Twelve slides of confidence, one working feature, and a teacher with raised eyebrows. 2.3.5 The Joke in the Code

Our class had a tradition nobody taught us: hiding jokes inside programs. A variable named after the mess uncle. An error message that said, please do not do this to me. 2.3.6 The Order Inside Chaos

People praise systems, plans and clean processes. My four years taught me to praise something else: productive chaos. Every good thing I built began as a mess — a joke on a lawn, a robot with no purpose, an error read aloud like poetry. The mess was not a failure of planning. It was the raw material that planning later refined.

I learnt to make room for it deliberately. One evening a week became unscheduled on purpose: no agenda, no goal, just the team and whatever idea wandered in. Half those evenings produced nothing but laughter, which was already something. The other half produced our strangest and best features. Order gives you what you asked for; chaos occasionally gives you what you needed.

So if your student life looks like a beautiful disaster — plans collapsing, projects silly, desks stormy, demos dying loudly — do not rush to fix all of it. Keep one foot in the mess. That is where the memories and the odd ideas live. Structure will come with age; it comes for everyone. The chaos years are the only ones that cannot be ordered later. Enjoy the storm while it is yours.

2.4.1 The One-Day Miracle

My first exam of engineering was approached with full scientific planning: I studied one entire day before it. Twenty-four hours of notes, chai and prayer, and I walked in with hope, fear, and one night of reading as my entire army. Then the paper began, and somehow, question after question, that single desperate day kept answering. My handwriting hit speeds it has never touched again.

The result came back passable, even decent, and that is where the real story starts. My brain filed a dangerous conclusion under proven methods: one day is enough. From that day on, the syllabus and I maintained a simple treaty — it stayed forgotten, I stayed busy, and we met properly only in the final seventy-two hours. It was a horrible system. It was also, embarrassingly, my system.

I do not recommend the one-day method to anyone, and I confess it worked for me more than once. Here is the honest analysis: cramming works exactly until it does not, and you never know which exam is the exception until you are sitting in it. I survived my luck. Many around me did not. Treat the one-day miracle as a lucky accident, never as a strategy. Luck does not take attendance.

2.4.2 The Three-Hour Formula

After the first exam set the precedent, a national standard emerged across our batch: three hours of panicked revision per paper. Not two, not four. Three. It was practically a laboratory finding, replicated across fifty students and eight semesters. In those three hours, a human being reads one semester at the speed of panic, and retains precisely enough. Enough, we learnt, is a moving target.

The three hours had a refined internal structure, passed down like a family recipe. Hour one: read everything once at speed, no notes, pure exposure. Hour two: attack the important questions, the ones every senior swears appear every year. Hour three: surrender to the probable — five questions, memorised cold, with full faith in the pattern of the universe and the paper-setter's laziness.

The funny thing is what those three hours taught me about my own brain. Pressure is a formatting tool: it deletes the decorative and keeps the core. Even today, before any big meeting or deadline, I give myself the same three-hour ritual — one pass over everything, one pass over what matters, one pass over what will definitely be asked. The exam changed. The formula stayed.

2.4.3 The Ratta Anthem

Every batch has an anthem, and ours was sung during revision, at full volume, on the terrace: Patak patak ke, rattaaaa maarrrr. Translation for outsiders: we beat the knowledge into our heads, together, rhythmically, and with far more joy than the phrase suggests. The terrace sessions had rules. One person read the definition aloud, the rest repeated it like a congregation, and the definition that survived three laps without anyone laughing became permanent. Dates, formulas, full forms — everything was converted into rhythm. Our marks owed a quiet debt to volume and repetition, and the neighbours owed us noise complaints they mercifully never filed.

People dismiss ratta as the lazy cousin of understanding, and sometimes they are right. But those evenings taught a deeper craft: how to compress. To fit a chapter into a chant, you must first understand it enough to know what to keep. The anthem was silly; the skill was not. Even today, when I prepare for anything big, I try to explain it in three sentences out loud. If it cannot be chanted, it is not yet clear.

2.4.5 The Jugaad Degree

Alongside the degree, engineering awarded us all a second, unofficial qualification: jugaad. The art of making things work with what is present. A laptop stand built from two matchboxes. My first job surprised me with how much of my value came from that habit. Big systems fail politely and wait for permission; jugaad minds fix things now with matchboxes. Keep the habit. It is the degree that never expires.

2.4.6 The Last-Minute Creed

Let me confess the truth of our batch with full clarity: we were never early. Not once, not for anything. Assignments, files, exam prep, train bookings — everything departed at the last minute, waving cheerfully. And yet, somehow, nothing was ever left undone. The last minute was not our enemy. So here is my creed, after years of arriving exactly on time by leaving exactly on time: respect your last minute, but never miss it. In engineering, and maybe in life, that sentence is a career.
